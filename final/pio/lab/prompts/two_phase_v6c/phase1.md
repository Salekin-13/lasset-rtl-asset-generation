THIS CALL: PHASE 1, CONCEPTUAL ASSETS

For each UNIT of the diagram:

1. Interface. Name the information entering or leaving through the unit's INPUTS and OUTPUTS: data received, held or returned; settings and configuration; state and status; commands, requests and their responses; events signaled to other modules.

2. Inside. Name state the unit holds that its behavior depends on and no port shows directly: a mode, a lock, a count, a stored setting, a state machine's state. Also name each enable, select, override or lock deciding whether other information is used, passes or may change: such a decision is information of its own. Neither sentence covers sequencing state (a rule below).

3. Merge and split. Information that always travels together for the same use, or repeats per index or per port of one role, is one piece. Information with different uses is separate, even on a shared port. An internal signal only timing one step of other information (an increment pulse, a started flag, a one-cycle request) is part of what it times, not a decision of its own. A compare of stored state with a request, a count with a stored limit, or a written value with a fixed value, whose result picks or triggers what happens next, is its own piece (step 2), even when it lasts one cycle. Hold it where it is computed; never merge it into its inputs or the action it triggers. Any other compare, decode or gate is judged by step 2 and the timing clause. A tick only pacing a step is a pacing pulse; a compare only picking a computation's result (a minimum or maximum) is a working value.

4. Rubric. Per piece, answer each question "yes" only when both its parts hold: first, a judgement of what the information is, from what the RTL does with it; second, a mechanism the RTL shows or the black-box rule (section 1) gives.
   Confidentiality. Assume the IP is to be integrated into an IC where confidentiality protections are required. Is this information, received as input or generated inside the IP, something an integrator may consider secret? If the first part holds, can an element of the IP leak or expose it, by holding or carrying it where the integration level can observe it: an output port, a read-back, a debug or test path, a status depending on it, a shared structure, or data-dependent timing (4.4)? Phase 2 judges authorization, so a designed use does not make the second part "no".
   Integrity. Assume the IP is to be integrated into an IC where integrity protections are required. Is this information material an integrator may deem sensitive, or a state or configuration setting that needs to be immutable during certain operations or modes? If the first part holds, can an element of the IP modify it?
   Availability. Does this information gate (enable, block or select) an output port or the use of an input port, or would the IP or the IC stop working without it? If the first part holds, can an element of the IP block, disable or withhold it, so that a port is blocked, a required transfer is lost, or the IP or IC halts?
   Undermined expected behavior. Is this information something that a privileged mode, an override, a bypass, test-packet injection or a similar behavior at the integration level acts on? If the first part holds, can that behavior make the IP produce incorrect output, or stop its function, under normal operation? Focus on what may be compromised.

5. Keep. Keep a piece as a conceptual asset when any question is "yes" AND it passes the asset test (2.3); list the rest under "Not Kept". Phase 2 sees only kept concepts and starts from their held lines.

6. Anchor. List under "Enters Through" each unit port whose value is carried (2.2) into the information, and under "Leaves Through" each unit port written by an element carrying it; either list may be empty. Skip ports that only decide the information or that it only decides. When a state machine, apart from reset, changes state only at steps a peer device's clock paces, and its next state depends only on its current state and one bit of a non-record input port (directly or through synchronizer stages), that port, never the peer's clock line, is the input port setting the state (2.6), though only conditions test its bit (an implicit flow, 2.2). So list that port under "Enters Through" of the concept holding that state; the skip above does not apply to it. Write ports as INPUTS and OUTPUTS do, or as the closed-set field when one field of a record port alone carries the information.

Rules:
- Describe a conceptual asset by what the information is, not by an element name. A diagram arrow shows a flow, not an asset.
- Precedence. A rule's bar is its sentence saying some information is not a conceptual asset or not a piece of its own. What a rule judges as usual or does not cover is spared only from that rule's bar.
- Clocks and resets. Clock and reset inputs, a reset request that the unit only combines, gates, registers or passes on to a reset chain or reset output port (its copies and that output port included), and chains only synchronizing or releasing a reset (a reset shift register, a registered copy of a reset), are not conceptual assets under any question. The clock line of a serial link that the unit drives to, or senses from, a peer device is not a conceptual asset either, nor is a setting or decision whose only use, apart from a read-back, is to shape that line (its polarity, its phase against the data, or holding it while the peer stretches it), although this rule judges other clock settings as usual. Do not cite their lines as held lines. The link's data and select lines, its rate setting and the unit's clock-enable outputs are judged as usual. A setting's only use is to shape a serial link's clock line also when, apart from its read-back, its counterparts show it gives value to or decides only the element driving that line, and that element gives value to or decides nothing but the line, even where the line's phase sets when the peer samples or sends data. It is then not a piece of its own: keep no concept for it and do not cite its lines as held lines; a transfer-mode, format or rate concept holding other settings stays, without it. Settings or requests controlling a clock (an enable, a rate) are judged as usual, and so is a decision triggering a reset (such as a timeout), where it is computed, not a copy of it that the unit only gates or registers, except as the next sentence says. A decision that a write was refused or carried a wrong value (a write while locked, a wrong password), its gated or registered copies, and a setting choosing only whether such a violation resets are not conceptual assets either when their only uses, apart from a read-back, are to request a reset and record its cause, although step 3 makes a compare of a written value with a fixed value a piece. The cause record, the lock refusing the write, and a compare outcome that restarts a counter are judged as usual.
- Build-time values. A generic or constant that only fixes the build (a feature switch, a size or depth, a fixed address or address map, a buffering choice) is not a conceptual asset: no element of the IP can change or withhold it at run time. Nor is a run-time bit that a statement ANDs with a build-time switch and whose effect includes writing characters to a file or the screen (a simulation-only mode), although a bus write can change it: it belongs to that build choice and is not the run-time decision the next sentence asks for. Instead, name the run-time decision using it as its own concept (such as which port an address selects, or which requester is granted).
- Pacing pulses. A tick, edge pulse or divider count made inside the unit only to pace another step, or an input port whose only use is a condition letting such a tick or count advance, is part of that step or its rate setting (step 3), unless an output port carries that pulse itself (2.2); an output port that such pulses only decide or time does not count. Do not name it in any concept's information or cite its lines as held lines. A request held until it is served, a start pulse decoded from a received command, and a step counter are not pacing pulses (sequencing state, below, judges a step counter).
- Forwarded transactions. A bus transaction (a request or response with its address, data and handshake) that the unit only forwards between its ports or to a sub-unit, without storing, computing or deciding it, is not a conceptual asset of this unit. Merging the bus responses of several ports into one (an OR or a multiplexer) is still forwarding. So is a register stage that captures a whole request or response record and passes it on unchanged a cycle later, although it stores the record. So is a record this unit only wires between one of its ports and the port map of an instance of another IP, with no statement of this unit reading or writing it (a constant tied to it in a generate branch where the instance is absent is no such statement), even though the black-box rule lets the instance change it. The decision whether or where it passes is judged as usual. Relayed data is data a transfer carries into or out of the unit (data loaded or stored, instruction words fetched, messages received or sent) when no statement of this unit stores it or computes from it: it only moves between a unit port and an instance of another IP, between such instances, or into a statement only mixing it with other values (an OR, a multiplexer). Relayed data, alone or with only the acknowledge and ready lines timing its transfer, is not a piece of its own, even in a unit executing instructions, although step 1 names data received or returned and 4.2 names end-user's private data: do not keep a concept for it. Judged as usual by this rule only: the address and direction of a transfer; a wait or fault flag; an interrupt or debug request; a register read-back word, and the register contents an instance holds; an arithmetic, logic or compare result an instance computes from operands; the value a mixing statement writes (such as the value written back to a register); and data the unit stores, shifts through its own register, generates or computes from, even when a buffer instance also holds it. Judge from port names which instance ports carry relayed data and which a computed result, and say so.
- Second copies. An internal register (not a port) that directly copies bits of one other register of its own entity, not as an index, whose other assignments write only constants, and that no condition reads, is a copy kept only while the value moves (2.6). It is not a piece of its own: name its information under the piece of the register it copies, and do not cite its lines as held lines.
- Bus handshake copies. A bus response acknowledge only repeating or delaying the bus request strobe, and a bus error flag tied to a constant, are not information of their own. In a unit passing bus requests from an input bus request port to an output one, a register of one bit, or one bit per requester, holds no information of its own when every assignment to it (directly or through its next-value signal), apart from clearing or holding it, copies or ORs in only the strobe, lock bit or response error bit of the access in progress, or sets it when a test reading only that access's fields and build-time values holds (a route bit), and the unit reads it only to start, pass on, route, time or end that access, the access it serves or that locked sequence, or to drop words that access fetched: the register only re-times a value the access already carries. It is not a piece of its own, although steps 1 to 4, Build-time values and 4.4 would name it as a request, status, lock, select, compare or bypass, and even where another rule judges a decision or a held request as usual. A compare or decode that reads a register this rule bars, and otherwise only that access's own fields and build-time values, or whose only use is to set such a register, is part of that register (step 3), not a piece of its own. Do not keep a concept for them, and do not cite their lines as held lines, except a line that also assigns an element this rule does not bar. Judged as usual: a held request read by a grant between requesters; the grant, its select and strobe; an address decode choosing which of several output bus request ports gets the access; a pending fence or flush clearing what the unit stores; a busy flag set when the unit itself issues an access; a timeout count, its error and its suppression flag; a reservation; a software-set lock guarding later accesses; a read enable deciding whether stored data leaves on the response; and a cache's hit. A one-bit register that one unconditional statement sets on every clock edge to a stored state bit or flag of this unit, copied or inverted and ANDed only with terms saying when it applies (a request strobe, its read or write bit, an operation-type decode), and that only a bus response's acknowledge or data fields read, only re-times that state to the response. It is part of that state, not a piece of its own, although step 1 names status and responses and step 3 makes a compare of stored state with a request a piece: do not keep a concept for it, and do not cite its lines as held lines. Judged as usual: that state, where held; a register set only from the unit's input ports (a read enable from a strobe and a read or write bit); and a flag that a condition sets to a constant (a timeout error raised at a count's limit).
- Working values. Partial results inside a computation (a partial sum, product or round value, a synchronizer stage, a debiasing step) are part of the result they produce (step 3), not concepts of their own. So are the state and flag made inside a debiasing step to choose which raw bits it accepts: although step 2 would call them a decision, they only shape that result, like a compare picking a minimum (step 3). Do not name a synchronizer stage in any concept's information or cite its lines as held lines.
- Slices. A signal (not a port) whose only assignment copies part of one element's bits (a slice), or joins signals each doing so from the same element, only carries that element's value (2.2), unless that element is a bus request or response record port or a field of one: such a slice is judged as usual (2.6). Otherwise it is not a piece of its own: do not cite its lines as held lines.
- Hand-offs. Check each port-map line where an instance's non-record output port drives a signal that another instance or this unit's logic reads. A wait, fault or write-enable flag, or an access address, that one instance outputs on a non-record port to another instance or this unit's logic is a hand-off; if the RTL cannot show which it is, judge from the port names at either end and say so; a field of a record an instance outputs is never a hand-off, even wired alone into another instance's non-record port. A hand-off is its own piece (step 2), unless Port buffers covers it (then this rule does not apply). No step-3 clause or other rule folds it, even when it travels with the data it qualifies, when another instance outputs the same address earlier or later (cite each output's port-map line, in one piece), or when it is only one bit of a count: then hold it at the line assigning that bit to the instance output, and a count kept only to make it belongs to this piece.
- Instance-driven records. A record driven whole by one instance of another IP and wired whole into one or more other instances' port maps (decoded controls or fetch results between sub-units; closed set: the record's own entry gets its value from one instance port and gives it only to instance ports) holds nothing of this unit, even if a port map also takes a field alone, a statement of this unit reads one, or it carries the instructions this unit executes: that instance holds each value; the record and its fields, one-bit flags included, only carry copies, and none is a non-record signal. So information that such a record or its fields carry, and that no other element, apart from bus request or response record ports, stores, computes or passes whole across the boundary (2.6; an instance output carrying it only by the black-box rule does not count), is not a piece of this unit, although step 2 names a state machine's state and each enable, and 2.4 and 4.2 name the privilege mode and the program counter: keep no concept for it. Information that a non-record unit input port itself brings in crosses the boundary whole at that port (2.6), even when the port is wired straight into that instance: list the port under "Enters Through" and hold the information at the line wiring that port. An element this unit's statements compute from such a field and other values keeps no concept about that field alive. Cite no declaration or port-map line of such a record, or of its fields, as a held line. Judged as usual: non-record signals handed between instances; non-record unit ports, even one wired straight into an instance (a debug or interrupt request line); records this unit's statements set, whole or per field; records whose fields port maps or several instances set separately; and, for its other values, an element this unit's statements compute from such a field and other values.
- Register read-back. In a unit executing instructions, the word an instance outputs for reads of its control and status registers, when a statement of this unit merges it directly into the value written back to a register, is its own piece: those registers' contents as software reads them. Hold it at that instance output. No step-3 clause or other rule folds it. In a unit executing instructions, a word that an instance outputs for reads of its own control and status registers, and that this unit reads only in an OR into the word another instance takes in to answer such reads, only carries those registers to the other instance's read-back: the registers (a count, a protection setting, a configuration or status bit) stay inside the first instance, and no statement of this unit stores, computes or decides them (a constant tied to the word in a generate branch lacking that instance is no such statement). So these words (that word and the one the OR makes) and those registers are not pieces of their own, although step 2 names a count and a stored setting and 2.4 a memory-protection configuration: keep no concept for them, as the read-back piece covers those registers as software reads them; cite no line of these words as a held line, nor, for the read-back piece, a port-map line feeding the first instance. Judge from port names (section 1's second exception) which outputs and inputs this rule names, and say so. Judged as usual: the read-back's instance output, the value written back to a register, and the first instance's other outputs (a wait or fault flag, a result, an access address, a unit output port it drives), even when computed from those registers.
- Port buffers. If the write data of a FIFO instance is a unit input port's value (a bus write, or bits shifted in from a serial input port), the FIFO only buffers data between two ports. Then its fill flags (whether it holds data or has room), its push and pop strobes, a command only clearing it, and a flag recording a push while full (even a register of this unit rather than an output of the FIFO) only time, gate, clear or report on that data: they are not pieces of their own, although step 2 would call some of them a decision. Judged as usual: the buffered data, an output port these flags decide (an interrupt request, a ready-to-receive line), and a unit enable that also clears the FIFO. A FIFO holding data the unit generates itself (random bits) is not covered: its fill flag and its push, pop, clear and enable controls are judged as usual. Any FIFO instance, whatever data it holds, keeps each word only while the word moves (2.6: a copy kept only while the value moves), so the signal wired to its write-data input and the signal its read-data output drives only carry the word; the buffered data is judged where the unit generates it (random bits) or where it crosses the unit's boundary. Keep the buffered data as a piece, but do not cite a line assigning or wiring those two signals as a held line, and leave "Held Lines" empty if no other line holds the data. Judge from port names that an instance is a FIFO, which outputs are its flags and which ports carry its data, and say so.
- Sequencing state. A state machine's state register (not a flag a request or command sets, held until that request is answered) or a bit, phase, word or step counter that restarts with each transfer or operation the unit performs (even if one bit copies an enable or a queued command picks the first step), and is read only to pick that transfer's next step, to time or gate its strobes, commands, shifts, loads, acknowledges and multiplexers, or to point its address at the next word, is sequencing state: it only orders steps of information other elements hold, so neither it, the busy flag decoded from it, nor the done flag it sets at its last step is information of its own. It and those flags are not pieces of their own, although step 2 names a state machine's state, a count and a select, and 2.4 names control flow (a program's): keep no concept for them, and do not cite their lines as held lines, except a line also assigning an element judged as usual. Judged as usual: any unit port (even an interrupt request these flags decide), the enable and rate settings, other elements these steps load or drive (a chip-select register, an address), the current grant (which requester holds a shared path), its select and strobe, a reservation one access sets and a later one checks, and a count whose compare with a limit raises a reset, error or interrupt, or sets an output level (a timeout or timer count), with that compare's flag and a timeout's suppression flag. Not covered, as their steps make the value or follow a peer's signals: a coprocessor, a unit shifting in bits it generates, and a test or debug access port that the peer's clock paces. In a coprocessor, a count that a multi-cycle operation steps every clock cycle of its busy state, and whose end test only leaves that state, is a step counter, one piece of information with that state. Hold it with the state machine's state, not with the operation's result or its pacing.
- Serial shift registers. In a unit shifting data bit by bit onto or off a serial data port, paced by the unit's own rate setting, the data shift register only holds each word while moving it between that port and the element taking or giving the whole word, so the port is where that data crosses the unit's boundary whole (2.6). Keep the data as a piece: list that port under "Enters Through" or "Leaves Through" and cite the lines reading or driving it as entry or exit lines, even when they name the shift register; do not cite a line assigning the shift register as a held line, and leave "Held Lines" empty if no other line holds the data.
- Buffer and engine status bits. A status bit a bus read returns is not information of its own when every element it copies (even inverted) or combines is barred by this call's rule on a FIFO only buffering data between two ports or on sequencing state: it only tells software whether the buffered or shifted data can move or was lost, which that data, its settings and the output ports these elements decide already cover. Although step 1 names status and 4.3 names status signals, keep no concept for such bits, alone or grouped as the status software reads, and do not cite the read-data lines returning them as held lines. A bit returning anything else, even in the same read word (a stored setting, selection or lock, the record of a reset's cause, a flag of a FIFO holding data the unit generates itself), is not covered: it belongs to the piece it returns, as that piece's exit (step 6). Not covered either: the output ports these elements decide (an interrupt request, a ready-to-receive line), a register that a test or debug access port shifts out, and, in a unit executing instructions, the word an instance outputs for reads of its control and status registers.
- Port options. A setting a bus write stores is a port option when, apart from its read-back, its counterparts show it gives value to or decides some element, and each is an interrupt request output (it picks which events raise it), a flow-control handshake output, or an element decided at a test letting a flow-control handshake input (or its synchronized copy) hold a transfer: it only tunes those ports, which carry the outcome. Judge from port names which ports are interrupt requests or flow-control handshakes, never a serial link's clock line, and say so. Judged as usual: those ports, and a setting with another such element (an enable also copied into engine state, clearing a buffer or driving a clock-enable output; a rate setting; a lock; a limit compared with a count). A port option is not a piece of its own, although step 2 names each enable, step 3 splits by use and it gates an output port: keep no concept for it, and do not name it in any concept's information or cite the lines assigning it as held lines. A concept also holding a port it tunes stays, for that port alone.
- Cached copies. In a cache (a unit answering a read from its own copy when a stored tag matches the address, and otherwise fetching the words over a backing bus), the copied words are copies of data the backing memory holds, and the tag and valid bits only record which words are copied: none of them is a piece of this unit, although steps 1 and 2 would name them and 4.4 names caches. Its decisions stay judged as usual: the hit, the bytes a write changes, the address, its fill, clear and invalidate commands, and a pending fence or flush. A memory that is itself the store is judged as usual.
- Held addresses. A register whose value, apart from constants and itself, comes only from part of an access's address field, directly or through its next-value wire or a wire only copying that part, and that only feeds other addresses or indexes a memory, holds that address only while the access is served (2.6: a copy kept only while the value moves). It and its next-value wire are part of the access address, not pieces of their own: do not cite their lines as held lines.
- Operation selectors. In a unit that executes an instruction or an atomic memory operation a processor issues to it (a coprocessor, an atomic read-modify-write), the instruction or request fields that only pick which operation runs or which result form returns (an opcode, a function code, an instruction-type bit), a stored copy of them, and a flag decoded from them that only names the operation are part of that operation (step 3), not pieces of their own, although step 2 names a select. Do not list them under "Enters Through" or cite their lines as held lines, except a line assigning an element this rule judges as usual. Judged as usual: a start command or pulse, even one decoded per operation (a multiply start, a divide start), a register access's address and write enable, a completion flag, and a setting decoded from them that changes how operands are read (such as signedness). A plain bus read or write is not covered: Access command fields judges its read or write bit and a read enable made from it. In a unit executing an instruction or atomic memory operation a processor issues to it, a register whose value, apart from constants and itself, comes only from one source operand input (a port or a request-record field) when the operation starts, directly or through its next-value wire, and reaches an output port only through the element holding the result, only holds that operand while the operation uses it (2.6: a copy kept only while the value moves). It and its next-value wire are part of that operand's piece: keep that piece, list the operand input under "Enters Through", cite the lines where it takes the operand as entry lines, and do not cite them as held lines.
- Access command fields. On a record port carrying accesses addressing this unit's own registers, FIFOs, locks or memory words (not a cache's copies), the fields only saying whether an access happens, its direction and which register, FIFO or bytes it reaches (a strobe, a read or write bit, register-select address bits, byte enables), and a write enable whose assignment reads only these fields and that only lets a register take the written data, are part of the information the access writes or reads, not information of their own. So they are not pieces of their own, although steps 1 and 2, Build-time values and Per-region checks would keep some of them: keep no concept for them, and cite no line that only tests them, or only assigns such a write enable, as a held line. A signal of its own decoded from these fields that gates or masks what a read returns (a read enable, a select also masking the read data), or pops a FIFO of data the unit generates itself, is its own piece (step 2): name it by what it decides and hold it at the line assigning it; no step-3 clause folds it. A condition written inline is no such signal, nor is a strobe of a FIFO that Port buffers covers. A register taking such a field's value as its new content holds that field's information, so the field belongs to its piece. Not covered: a memory address indexing words; a request held until served, a select routing it on, and a strobe driven on to a unit output port; an operation's start pulse; and every port that is not a record port.
- Per-region checks. When the unit makes the same compare or permission check per region or entry and only combines the results (a priority chain, an OR) into one decision, that decision is the piece, held where the results combine; the per-region results are its working values (step 3), although step 3 makes a compare of stored state with a request a piece. A per-entry result that itself picks where a request goes or which entry an access changes (which port an address selects, which lock is addressed) stays its own piece.
- Arbitration bookkeeping. A register only remembering the previous grant for round-robin priority holds no decision of its own: it only tilts the next grant. It is not a piece of its own, even though step 2 names a select: do not keep a concept for it, and do not cite its lines as held lines. Judged as usual: the current grant, its select and strobe.
- Processor context fields. A field, of a record input port other than a bus request or response record port (2.6), that tells the processor's privilege mode, whether it is in debug mode or takes a trap, or whether the current access is a fetch or data access, read or write, only refines a decision this unit computes from other information when this unit only tests it (a condition, select, compare with a fixed value, gate on another result), directly or through a wire only selecting between such fields: the processor holds that state; the record carries a copy. Such a field, and such a wire, are part of the decision they refine (step 3), not pieces of their own, although steps 1 and 2 would name them and 2.4, 4.2 and 4.4 point at privilege and debug modes: do not keep a concept for them, list them under "Enters Through" or cite their lines as held lines, except a line assigning an element this rule judges as usual. Judged as usual: the decision such a field refines, where computed or stored (a permission verdict, fault output, execution state); the access address, compared with stored state; and the address, write enable and data of a write to a register of this unit. Not covered: the whole record port, a non-record input port (a peer's flow-control line, an interrupt or debug request), and a port or field wired into an instance. Judge what a field tells from port field names and their uses; say so.
